SPECIFIC AIMS
Precise temporal control of gene expression, a requisite for neurons to adopt and maintain their identity during development, takes place at both the levels of both chromatin (histone post-translational modifications) and DNA (methylation). For example, histone ubiquitination and acetylation help to determine how accessible chromatin structure is to cell-specific transcription factors. The Ferguson lab’s recent studies of histone modifications in neurons have revealed surprising new ways of thinking about these epigenetic regulators and how they operate during neurodevelopment. This proposal builds upon this foundation.
Our lab studies ubiquitination of histone H2A (specifically H2AK119ub, hereafter H2Aub)1, which is placed by the Polycomb Repressive Complex 1 (PRC1). Loss of PRC1 function de-represses genes in the context of various cancers, but it has not been clear how mutations in components of PRC1 or the related Polycomb Repressive Complex 2 (PRC2) cause inherited neurodevelopmental disorders. Mapping H2Aub (along with other histone marks) in the mouse cerebellum at early postnatal and adult timepoints revealed that this ‘silencing’ modification becomes depleted from repressed genes and redirected toward active enhancers and promoters1. These findings overturned the textbook view that Polycomb Complexes act only at repressed loci. The Ferguson lab took the next logical step, asking how loss of function mutation of the H2Aub deubiquitinase BAP1, which also causes a human neurodevelopmental disorder2, affects neurodevelopment. Since global loss of Bap1 is lethal, we generated neuron-specific Bap1 conditional mutant mice that exhibit the developmental delay, seizures, and behavioral abnormalities seen in patients. Using CUT&RUN and transcriptomics, we found that euchromatic and heterochromatic regions come to more closely resemble one another: euchromatic genes exhibited reduced gene expression (due to more H2Aub, more PRC2-dependent H3K27me3, and less of the activating modification H3K27ac), while heterochromatic loci developed the opposite pattern, resulting in gene overexpression. These changes emerged only in adult neurons, suggesting that BAP1 and its substrate H2Aub control gene expression during the prolonged process of post-mitotic chromatin maturation.
In addition to BAP1’s regulation of histone modifications, there is reason to believe BAP1 and H2Aub also control DNA methylation: previous biochemical studies showed that H2Aub interacts with the methylation ‘writer’ DNMT3A3–6. To probe the functional significance of this potentially highly consequential interaction in neurons, we asked if BAP1 loss alters neuronal DNA methylation. We used the Biomodal EvoC kit to simultaneously profile cytosine methylation and hydroxymethylation (5mC and 5hmC) in adult mutant neurons and found reciprocal methylation changes that augment changes to histone modifications: euchromatic loci exhibited higher 5mC (generally associated with repression) and lower 5hmC (associated with activation), whereas heterochromatic loci exhibited lower 5mC and higher 5hmC. We also found that MeCP2 (which reads methylated cytosines) is redistributed along with H2Aub changes at neuronal genes, particularly those associated with synapse formation and function. We hypothesize that H2Aub recruits DNMT3A to methylate DNA such that abnormal distribution of H2Aub (as in the absence of BAP1) will cause parallel changes at the level of DNA methylation. The objective of this project is therefore to determine whether BAP1, through H2Aub, regulates the neuronal DNA methylation landscape and by what mechanism. Because BAP1 loss perturbs H2Aub bidirectionally, we have an ideal system to test its relationship with DNA methylation in the following two aims.
Aim 1: Establish the sequence of H2AK119ub and DNA methylation changes after BAP1 loss and determine whether these methylation changes require DNMT3A. We will map H2Aub (CUT&RUN) and profile DNA methylation using the Biomodal evoC kit across maturation timepoints (P7, P12, P21, adult). This time course tests our hypothesis directly: if H2AK119ub instructs methylation, its redistribution should precede or co-occur with methylation changes; if methylation changes appear first, this would indicate independent epigenetic regulation. While most DNA methylation occurs at CG dinucleotides (mCG), neuronal DNMT3A also deposits 5mC in a non-CG context (mCH), regulating neuronal gene expression in myriad ways7–9. We have established the capability to detect non-CG methylation using the Enzymatic Methyl-seq method coupled with specific informatics tools and will assess the effect of Bap1 loss on this neuronally enriched form of methylation. In parallel, we will cross a conditional Dnmt3a knockout onto the Bap1 mutant background to test whether removing DNMT3A blocks changes in 5mCG and 5mCH at euchromatic sites of H2Aub gain.
Aim 2: Determine whether BAP1 catalytic-domain mutations remodel DNA methylation through H2Aub in human neurons. BAP1 missense variants in patients typically disturb the function of the catalytic ubiquitin C-terminal hydrolase (UCH) domain. We are introducing two patient UCH-domain mutations into WTC11 human iPSCs bearing a doxycycline-inducible NGN2 transgene that drives homogeneous differentiation into excitatory neurons. We will compare H2Aub, H3K27ac, and H3K27me3 with transcriptomics and DNA methylation (Biomodal evoC; Enzymatic Methyl-seq; MeCP2 CUT&RUN). If H2Aub recruits DNMT3A, loss of BAP1 deubiquitinase activity will raise H2Aub and reproduce the 5mC/5hmC changes seen in mouse neurons. Alternatively, changes to histone modifications but not to DNA methylation would demonstrate the independence of these epigenetic mechanisms. Because non-CG methylation accrues slowly, we will extend differentiation in culture to compare against the time course in Aim 1. Together, these aims move from correlation to causation and establish a human platform for studying BAP1-related neuronal dysfunction.
